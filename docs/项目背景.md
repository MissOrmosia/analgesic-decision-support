# 项目背景（详细版）

> 本文是 README 背景的完整版，讲述这个项目从何而来、为什么长成现在这个样子。

## 一、临床日常：疼痛管理是我的工作

我是一名工作了 5 年的三甲医院外科医生。我的日常工作包括围手术期管理和癌症病人管理，疼痛管理是其中非常重要的一环。

虽然我不是疼痛科医生，但：

- **术后疼痛**我要管——手术病人禁食期、恢复口服后的镇痛阶梯都由我决策；
- **晚期癌痛**我要管——癌症病人的长期镇痛方案调整是病房日常；
- **胆绞痛**我要管——肝胆胰外科的痉挛性疼痛是家常便饭；
- **急诊坐诊**时，还会遇到大量泌尿系疾病（结石等）导致的痉挛痛病人；
- 偶尔还会遇到抵抗力低下的病人出现**带状疱疹**，继而发展为带状疱疹后神经痛。

## 二、知识成型的契机：一次科室小讲课

关于止痛药的知识，最初是零散地在我脑子里成型的。直到有一次科室小讲课，我以止痛药的使用为主题，把它们分门别类地系统讲解了一遍：

1. **非甾体类抗炎药（NSAIDs）**：再细分为非选择性 COX 抑制剂和选择性 COX-2 抑制剂（如塞来昔布、依托考昔）；
2. **阿片类药物**：曲马多、芬太尼类、吗啡类等；
3. **神经病理性疼痛用药**：加巴喷丁、普瑞巴林（带状疱疹后神经痛）；
4. **其他类型**：如对乙酰氨基酚——很多人以为它是 NSAIDs，从药物机制上其实并不是；
5. **解痉药**：间苯三酚、屈他维林，在痉挛性疼痛中很好用。

## 三、观察到的痛点

在整理和讲解的过程中，我发现很多医生对止痛药的使用**非常粗糙**：常见的几种药来回切换，实在不行就找疼痛科会诊。

找疼痛科会诊本身是对的，但现实中：

- 你不一定有条件**迅速**得到会诊意见；
- **夜班场景**尤其需要你马上做出决策；
- 粗糙用药的背后，是禁忌筛查的缺位——很少有人在开止痛药前系统过一遍年龄、消化道基础病、肾功能。

## 四、从决策树文档到 AI 助手

求职时，我根据一个岗位的要求做了一份**止痛药决策树文档**：包含止痛药分类表格，以及术后疼痛、晚期癌痛的阶梯决策路径。这份文档就是我最初用于 vibe coding 这个止痛药助手的蓝本。

做出 AI 版的止痛药助手之后，我反复测试，发现了各种各样的问题——医学类的、交互体验类的、代码类的——然后逐一解决。最终累计记录了 103 项问题（见 docs/问题日志.md），也沉淀出了一套完整的设计依据。

## 五、设计依据：为什么这样设计

### 1. 小儿用药必须严格分层

很多止痛药小儿不能用，查说明书就可以查到。即便小儿可以用，也往往**区分年龄段**（如曲马多 12 岁以下禁用、间苯三酚限 2 岁以上），有时甚至**区分剂型**——比如对乙酰氨基酚的片剂不推荐 6 岁以下儿童，应选用滴剂或混悬液。

→ 对应设计：小儿精确年龄输入、2/6/12 岁阈值判断、剂型警示、小儿用药白名单。

### 2. NSAIDs 的胃肠道风险必须门控

非甾体类抗炎药（特别是非选择性 COX-2 抑制剂）会造成一定程度的胃肠道黏膜损伤。在胃十二指肠溃疡、克罗恩病、溃疡性结肠炎患者身上，可能加重原有病情，甚至被说明书列为禁忌。

→ 对应设计：NSAIDs 筛查弹窗门控，合并症路径自动从阶梯中移除 NSAIDs。

### 3. 肾功能决定一切

很多止痛药经肾脏代谢或具有肾毒性，肾功能不全的病人需要减量甚至禁用。

→ 对应设计：全局必经的肾功能筛查（含未查三态处理、五层分层、Scr/eGFR 手动计算、儿童 Schwartz 公式）。

### 4. 高危场景下，AI 应该把医生推向会诊

小儿叠加意外情况、中度以上肾功能损伤，都是典型的高危场景。我们**不鼓励医生在高危场景下自己决定用药**——这是医疗安全和规避法律风险的最优解。

AI 助手可以提供建议，但如果医生因为助手的建议而轻敌大意、忽略了向专科人士寻求医疗资源，这种思路是很致命的。

→ 对应设计：三级安全闸门（警示 / 建议会诊 / 强制会诊），高危场景下本助手宁可不推荐任何药物。

### 5. 交互必须低负荷、防敷衍

我自己就是临床医生，临床上非常繁忙，所以我希望所有交互都是**简洁**的，不要耗费太多脑容量——如果这个助手用起来很复杂或很难用，我自己就会弃用。

而且正因为繁忙，在强制会诊等需要填写理由的环节，如果没有审计校验，医生（包括我）有充分的理由胡写乱写。

→ 对应设计：全部点选式交互、理由填写的汉字校验与审计留痕、"取消"不留痕的暗道全部封堵。

## 六、协作方式说明

本项目由我本人提出全部临床需求与测试反馈，通过两个 AI 协作完成开发：一个负责代码生成，一个负责代码评审与自动化回归测试。我本人作为调度者，负责判断哪个 AI 的输出可信、在意见冲突时做裁决、并对每一条医学规则的正确性负最终责任。

103 项问题中的每一项，都来自我在真实使用场景中的测试与追问。

# 🇬🇧 English Version

# Project Background (Detailed)

> This is the full version of the README background — how the project came about and why it took its current shape.

## 1. Clinical Routine: Pain Management Is My Job

I am a surgeon at a tertiary Grade-A hospital in China with 5 years of experience. My daily work covers perioperative management and cancer patient management, in which pain management is a critical component.

Although I am not a pain specialist:

- **Postoperative pain** is mine to manage — the analgesic ladders for surgical patients during the fasting period and after resuming oral intake are my decisions;
- **Advanced cancer pain** is mine to manage — adjusting long-term analgesic regimens for cancer patients is routine ward work;
- **Biliary colic** is mine to manage — spasmodic pain is everyday fare in hepatobiliary-pancreatic surgery;
- During **ER shifts**, I see large numbers of colic patients from urinary diseases (stones, etc.);
- Occasionally, immunocompromised patients develop **herpes zoster**, which can progress to postherpetic neuralgia.

## 2. How the Knowledge Took Shape: A Department Teaching Session

My knowledge of analgesics first formed in scattered fragments. It came together during a department teaching session where I gave a systematic lecture on analgesic use, classifying them into:

1. **NSAIDs** — further divided into non-selective COX inhibitors and selective COX-2 inhibitors (e.g., celecoxib, etoricoxib);
2. **Opioids** — tramadol, fentanyl-class agents, morphine, etc.;
3. **Neuropathic pain agents** — gabapentin, pregabalin (for postherpetic neuralgia);
4. **Other types** — such as acetaminophen: many assume it is an NSAID, but mechanistically it is not;
5. **Antispasmodics** — phloroglucinol, drotaverine, highly effective for spasmodic pain.

## 3. The Pain Points I Observed

While preparing the lecture, I noticed that many physicians use analgesics **very crudely**: rotating through a few common drugs, and calling a pain consult when all else fails.

Calling a pain consult is the right thing to do, but in reality:

- You may not be able to get consult input **quickly**;
- **Night shifts** demand immediate decisions;
- Behind the crude prescribing lies absent contraindication screening — few people systematically check age, gastrointestinal comorbidities, and renal function before writing an analgesic order.

## 4. From Decision-Tree Document to AI Assistant

While applying for a job, I prepared a **painkiller decision-tree document** per one position's requirements: classification tables plus ladder decision paths for postoperative pain and cancer pain. That document became the blueprint for vibe-coding this assistant.

After building the AI version, I tested it relentlessly and found problems of every kind — medical, interaction-experience, and code — and fixed them one by one. The process ultimately logged 103 issues (see docs/issue-log.md) and produced a complete design rationale.

## 5. Design Rationale: Why It Is Built This Way

### 1. Pediatric medication must be strictly stratified

Many analgesics are contraindicated in children — the labels state this plainly. Even when usable, they are often **stratified by age band** (e.g., tramadol contraindicated under 12; phloroglucinol restricted to over 2), and sometimes **by formulation** — acetaminophen tablets, for instance, are not recommended for children under 6, who should use drops or suspension instead.

→ Corresponding design: precise pediatric age input, 2/6/12-year threshold logic, formulation warnings, and a hard-coded pediatric medication whitelist.

### 2. NSAID gastrointestinal risk must be gated

NSAIDs (especially non-selective COX inhibitors) cause a degree of gastrointestinal mucosal injury. In patients with gastroduodenal ulcer, Crohn's disease, or ulcerative colitis, they may worsen the underlying condition and are listed as contraindicated in drug labels.

→ Corresponding design: NSAID screening as a modal gate; comorbidity paths automatically strip NSAIDs from the ladder.

### 3. Renal function decides everything

Many analgesics are renally metabolized or nephrotoxic; patients with renal impairment need dose reduction or outright avoidance.

→ Corresponding design: globally mandatory renal screening (three-state unchecked handling, five-tier stratification, manual Scr/eGFR calculation, Schwartz formula for children).

### 4. In high-risk scenarios, the AI should push the physician toward a consult

Children with additional complications, or moderate-to-severe renal impairment, are typical high-risk scenarios. We **do not encourage physicians to make medication decisions alone** in high-risk situations — this is the optimal strategy for both patient safety and medico-legal risk mitigation.

An AI assistant may offer suggestions, but if a physician, emboldened by the assistant's advice, neglects to escalate to specialist resources, the consequences can be fatal.

→ Corresponding design: a three-tier safety gate (warning / recommended consult / mandatory consult); in high-risk scenarios the assistant would rather recommend no drug at all.

### 5. Interactions must be low-load and perfunctory-proof

I am a clinician myself, and clinicians are extremely busy. I want every interaction to be **simple** and to consume as little mental bandwidth as possible — if the assistant were complicated or hard to use, I would abandon it myself.

And precisely because we are busy, in mandatory-consult scenarios requiring written reasons, without audit validation a physician (myself included) has every incentive to write nonsense.

→ Corresponding design: fully tap-through interactions, Chinese-character validation with audit trails for written reasons, and every unaudited "cancel" backdoor sealed.

## 6. How the Collaboration Worked

I raised all clinical requirements and test feedback myself; two AIs collaborated on development — one for code generation, one for code review and automated regression testing. I acted as orchestrator: deciding whose output to trust, arbitrating conflicts, and bearing final responsibility for the correctness of every medical rule.

Every one of the 103 issues came from my testing and probing in real usage scenarios.
